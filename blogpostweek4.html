A few days ago I was listening to a podcast “That Daily Brain Rot Is Taking Us Back to the Dark Ages” from the New York Times “Interesting Times,” where it was a conversation with James Marriott about the post-literate society and the effects our contemporary media consumption has on both us as the individual members of society and our society at large, that is the rise of democracy as the main governing political system that came with the advent of text based society and the growth in literacy. When reading Claire Bishop’s argument about the Italian Futurism’s main characteristics I was taken back how similar the trends we are experiencing right now resemble the features of art—and broader societal implications behind them—that the Futurists advocated for. Tying the two together, Marriott talks about how the decrease of literacy—in particular long form reading—are the other side of the coin of the rise of visual media, that is, the image. Reading long-form texts like books and even essays demand focus, attention and time from the readers that in our increasingly screen-mediated world is something that is scorned or at least thought of as a waste, when you can watch an instagram reel, or a tiktok or a youtube short that will provide you with the essential information, already processed and packaged for the delivery to you. The consequences are obvious: the consumers are not encouraged to think, to interpret and to analyze the given information critically. Therefore, the form of media consumption today is fast and effective in forming masses and online crowds that think alike, or rather have been made to think alike. Bishop’s mention of Le Bon’s argument of the crowds where the emphasis is on “images rather than logical discourse for communicating with crowds,” is exactly the argument that Marriott is making, but the latter is also introducing the political angle, saying that this rise of image-based society is drastically decreasing the democratic foundations of our society which are the logical discourse and the ability for an individual critical thinking that builds from our engagement with texts that are not processed and packaged for the short form consumption. What social media affords is the element of participation that the Futurists desired; anyone can be part of the action and part of the crowd. Of course, the romantic ideal of social media as the platform bringing people together and being the cornerstone of democratic principles by providing everyone with a voice is still alive, it is being exploited and turned into the means for prolifilating hostile, antagonistic and hateful content that unites people and affords them the feeling that they are heard, they are participating. The intention to provoke conflict that the Italian Futurists’ performances had is being realized, in different scale and for arguably different reasons, on the stage of social media today. To incite people and create the sense of connection between the audience members with the goal “to produce a space of participation as one of total destruction” is easier to achieve with images rather than burnedsome, time-consuming, intellectually-demanding activity such as reading.
