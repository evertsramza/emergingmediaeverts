Week 1 blog post: Throughout the essay Steyerl employs the concept of “resolution” to relate to the quality of an image—either high or low resolution indicative of the quality of an image—and every image is part of the “class society of appearances,” where it is “ranked according to its resolution.” Due to their status as being “illicit or degraded” the poor images are not assigned any value, but it is not the case that they are simply given the lowest possible value, but instead their status “grants them exemption from its [the system’s] criteria.” The criteria that Steyerl refers to is the resolution as in the quality of the image. However, when Steyerl turns to the “neoliberal restructuring of media production,” the repercussions of that are that non-commercial imagery have been further obscured and almost eliminated from the circulation of images in mainstream media. In this context, the other meaning of resolution, i.e., conclusion or outcome, could be viewed as the new, or the supplementary, criteria that the neoliberal restructuring of media production emphasized in the revised approach to the evaluation of images. By that I mean that if an image did not have the desired resolution—a resolution that would not align with the neoliberal commercial demands, which would imply that the image was not inherently produced to become a commodity—it would be considered a poor image because it does not reflect the values—or rather demands—of the neoliberal media production system. Following this newly formed understanding of an image’s resolution as the guiding criteria for the evaluation of an image, it is not only the loss of the visual substance that recovers an image’s “political punch and creates a new aura around it,” but the resolution of the image, its non-alignment or outright disregard for the commercial imperative of the neoliberal system of media production, that inverts the “poorness” of the image. The image becomes a rich image because it affords multiple directions of interpretation and its possible uses are not restricted to merely being a commodity; it can be reappropriated and disseminated for different purposes and with different meanings. The pervasive thought that I simply could not shake off while reading Steyerl’s piece was how this understanding of resolution—both in terms of quality and desired outcome—is complicated by the invention of A.I. and the image making tools that it provides. No longer people have to choose, or rather satisfice, to work with poor images because they don’t have the resources to produce a rich and stunning, and thus expensive, image. Anybody can produce—of course it won’t be them producing, but the chatbot—high-resolution (quality) image now, but isn’t anything that A.I. produces a poor image in the sense that it is part of one hughe pile of A.I. slop?
