The first thought I had while reading the piece was about the character of Ava, the AI robot, in the film Ex Machina by Alex Garland. I know that Ava is not a cyborg, she doesn’t have the organic—human—aspect that constitutes a cyborg, she is all artificial, all robot, all AI. But the reason why I thought of the film and that character is that, in my mind, AI robots that are made to look like humans, for now purely science fiction, are attempts to bypass the category of cyborgs and go from all artificial to all organic, at least for the people who interact with them without the knowledge of their artificiality. What I mean by all organic is that these robots are attempts to pass as humans, that’s the whole thing about the Turing test: an interactive test that should decide whether the person engaging in a conversation with an AI can tell that the “being” on the other side is a robot and not a person. And as with the situation we’re in today, 41 years after Donna Haraway wrote A Cyborg Manifesto, the part where she writes that “Our machines are disturbingly lively” has only increased in its truthfulness. Embracing the concept of cyborgs and a cyborg world as the path of possibility resulting in a world “in which people are not afraid of their joint kinship with animals and machines, not afraid of permanently partial identities and contradictory standpoints,” I’m not sure if that would mean us as humans succumbing to the reality of AI’s “inevitable” role in our lives, where we have to become part humans part machines, or that would mean adapting to this reality and by doing so resisting the total take over by the machines in the form of AI robots like Ava. I guess I’m rephrasing the two perspective dilemma with which Haraway ends the “An Ironic Dream of a Common Language for Women in the Integrated Circuit," part in a way that refers to the position that we’re in today.
